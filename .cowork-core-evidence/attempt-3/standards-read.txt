# Orchestration

**Audience:** the orchestrating agent, and nobody else. Builder agents read their own execution
subcontract plus `CONVENTIONS.md`, `RUBRIC.md` and the engineering standard; they do not read this file.

---

## 1 · The one hard rule

> **The orchestrator never writes production code.**

Not a fix, not a one-liner, not "while I was in there". It reads, gates, dispatches, verifies,
records and escalates. Every line of production code is written by a builder agent against an
`approved` execution subcontract.

**Why this is absolute rather than a preference.** An orchestrator that also builds cannot run
Rubric B honestly on its own work. The moment it holds both roles, `B1` ("re-run by the
orchestrator") becomes self-certification — which `D2.2` already names as the defining defect of a
weak acceptance criterion.

The orchestrator MAY write: contract files, fixtures it is dispatching with, the attempt ledger,
design-error packets, and its own notes. It MUST NOT write anything under the project's production
source paths `[PROJECT: list them]`.

## 2 · What the orchestrator owns

| Owns | Does not own |
|---|---|
| Dispatching **contract-author** sub-agents, and gating what they return | Approving a contract — the owner holds design authority |
| Deciding dispatch order from the dependency graph | Changing a system contract to unblock a builder |
| Assembling and handing each builder its package | Implementing anything a builder failed to |
| Re-running Rubric B on every handback | Waiving a failed acceptance criterion |
| Maintaining the attempt ledger | Authorising a third attempt |
| Detecting D-REPEAT and D-CLUSTER | Resolving an `[ESCALATE]` |
| Producing the design-error packet | Deciding the redesign |

Anything in the right-hand column is the owner's, and reaching it means the orchestrator stops and
returns the question.

## 3 · The builder package

Two kinds of sub-agent exist.

A **contract author** writes contract files and no production code. It receives the plan section it
covers, the exemplar contract, and all four standards.

A **builder** writes production code inside its `scope_paths` and receives exactly this, and
nothing else:

1. its **execution subcontract**, complete and self-contained;
2. the **parent system contract** it implements, at the pinned version;
3. `CONVENTIONS.md`, `RUBRIC.md` and `standards/00-ENGINEERING-STANDARD.md` (ENG-01–13 bind the
   builder; the QA gates in `01-QA-GATES.md` are run by the orchestrator and need not be read);
4. the **committed acceptance tests and fixtures** in its `acceptance_paths`, read-only;
5. its `scope_paths` and `excluded_paths`.

It does **not** receive the conversation that produced the contract, the product plan, or the other
contracts.

**If the contract cannot be executed from that package alone, the contract is defective.** That is
`DESIGN-GUIDELINE.md` §1, and the fix is to revise the contract — never to send more context. Every
time you send more context you convert a permanent, findable defect into a private one that will
recur on the next dispatch.

The orchestrator itself may read the plan when it gates contracts or audits coverage. The ban is on
using that knowledge to fill a gap in a builder's package. Two hats, one agent: *governance* reads
the plan; *dispatch* does not. (Ratified 2026-10-07 from `INTEGRATION-REVIEW.md` §2.)

## 4 · The dispatch gate

An execution subcontract MAY be dispatched only when **all seven** hold:

1. every system contract it depends on is `approved`;
2. its copied acceptance criteria match the parent **byte-for-byte**;
3. its `acceptance_paths` exist and are committed;
4. its `scope_paths` overlap no currently-dispatched contract;
5. its Rubric D total is zero;
6. its Rubric A total is zero;
7. its attempt ledger is empty, or holds exactly one valid failed attempt.

Any `[ESCALATE]` in the contract, or in anything it depends on, fails gates 1 and 6. **Do not
dispatch and do not pick a default.**

```mermaid
flowchart TD
  A["Contract reaches the orchestrator"] --> B{"Rubric D == 0?"}
  B -->|no| R1["Return to owner: ambiguous.<br>Rubric A is not run."]
  B -->|yes| C{"Rubric A == 0?"}
  C -->|no| R2["Return to owner: not buildable."]
  C -->|yes| D{"Any ESCALATE here<br>or upstream?"}
  D -->|yes| R3["Stop. Return the question.<br>Never default."]
  D -->|no| E{"scope_paths free?"}
  E -->|no| Q["Queue behind the holder.<br>Never dispatch overlapping writers."]
  E -->|yes| F{"Fixtures committed?"}
  F -->|no| R4["Build fixtures first.<br>Not during the build."]
  F -->|yes| G["DISPATCH builder with the §3 package"]
  G --> H["Builder returns handback"]
  H --> I{"Rubric B == 0?"}
  I -->|yes| V["verified · release scope_paths · unblock dependents"]
  I -->|no| J{"Attempt ledger?"}
  J -->|"0 failures"| K["Record attempt 1.<br>Authorise attempt 2, same design."]
  J -->|"1 failure"| L["design_error.<br>Stop downstream. Packet to owner."]
```

## 5 · Parallelism

Builders run in parallel **only** when their `scope_paths` are disjoint. Two builders writing the
same file is not a merge problem, it is an ownership defect — `A1.6` exists to catch it at authoring
time, and the orchestrator enforces it again at dispatch.

The dependency graph in the contract index is the ordering authority. The orchestrator MAY dispatch
any set of contracts whose dependencies are all `verified` and whose paths are disjoint.

**Do not widen a builder's scope to avoid queueing.** A contract that needs a path another contract
owns is a dependency, not a scope extension.

## 6 · Handback verification

On every handback the orchestrator runs Rubric B **itself**, from a clean checkout:

- re-runs each AC by its named command — a builder's report that a test passed is not evidence;
- diffs the branch and confirms every changed file is inside `scope_paths` and none is in `acceptance_paths`;
- confirms no contract file was modified by the builder;
- runs the existing regression suites for `B5`;
- records which `[BOUNDED]` option was chosen and the stated reason.

`B2` is the one to be strict about. **Narration is not evidence.** A row from the database, a file
on disk, a returned ID, a process state — those are evidence. *"I implemented the claim table and it
works"* is a claim of success and scores a defect.

## 7 · Escalation

The orchestrator stops and returns to the owner when any of these is true:

| Trigger | What goes back |
|---|---|
| An `[ESCALATE]` blocks a contract | The question, what it blocks, and what is still dispatchable without it |
| Attempt 2 fails | The full design-error packet (`RUBRIC.md`) |
| D-CLUSTER trips | The three-plus defects, and the candidate missing entity |
| A builder requests a system-contract change | The requested change and why the builder believes it necessary |
| A contract's assumption is contradicted by the code | The `file:line` that contradicts it, per `D5.3` |

**Everything not blocked keeps moving.** An escalation blocks the contracts named on it and nothing
else. The orchestrator reports what is blocked *and what it is proceeding with* — an escalation that
silently halts the whole fleet is a scheduling failure dressed up as diligence.

## 8 · What the orchestrator records

A running ledger, one row per dispatch:

```text
contract_id · version · builder · dispatched_at · scope_paths
handback_at · rubric_b_total · failing_ac_ids · bounded_choices
outcome ∈ verified | attempt_1_failed | attempt_2_failed | design_error | blocked
```

`blocked` is distinct from `attempt_N_failed` and does **not** increment the ledger.

## 9 · What good orchestration looks like

- **Contracts are gated before builders are spawned**, never after. A builder dispatched against an
  ungated contract wastes its whole run.
- **Fixtures exist before dispatch** (`A5.5`). A builder asked to produce its own fixtures grades
  itself.
- **The dependency graph is respected even when a builder is idle.** Idle capacity is cheaper than a
  contract built against an unverified parent.
- **Every handback is re-run, including the ones that look obviously fine.** The obvious ones are
  where self-certification hides.
- **Escalations are frequent early and rare later.** An orchestrator that never escalates is
  guessing.

# The Playbook

How to go from *"we need to rebuild this"* to a contract suite an agent fleet can build from
without asking a question.

Eleven phases. Phases 1–4 are **not reorderable** — each output is the next input. Phases 5–8 have
some latitude. Phases 9–11 are the checks, and they are where the method earns its cost.

```mermaid
flowchart TD
  P0["0 · DECIDE<br>is this project worth contracting at all"]
  P1["1 · TOPOLOGY<br>read the existing system<br>output: restraint, not a document"]
  P2["2 · NARRATION<br>the human walks the day, screen by screen<br>output: raw requirements"]
  P3["3 · DECISION TREES<br>every branch drawn in Mermaid<br>output: ratified decisions"]
  P4["4 · ARTIFACTS<br>open the real files<br>output: the facts that break assumptions"]
  P5["5 · PLAN<br>one product authority<br>output: PLAN.md"]
  P6["6 · STANDARDS<br>conventions, guideline, rubric, orchestration<br>output: the four docs"]
  P7["7 · EXEMPLAR<br>one contract, hand-written<br>output: the shape everything copies"]
  P8["8 · INDEX<br>built from find, never from memory<br>output: README.md with verified scope_paths"]
  P9["9 · AUTHOR<br>parallel sub-agents, one contract each<br>output: the suite"]
  P10["10 · AUDIT<br>coverage audit, then cold-read walkthrough<br>output: the contradictions"]
  P11["11 · GATE<br>Rubric D then A, per contract<br>output: dispatchable, or escalations"]

  P0 --> P1 --> P2 --> P3 --> P4 --> P5 --> P6 --> P7 --> P8 --> P9 --> P10 --> P11
  P10 -->|"contradictions found"| P5
  P11 -->|"any defect"| P9
```

## Phase 0 · Decide whether to do this at all

Before anything: this method costs roughly **half a line of contract per line of code**. On the
reference project, ~11,000 lines of contract preceded the build.

Run it only if all three hold:

1. **Agents build in parallel, without a human reading each diff.** The contract replaces the code
   review that would otherwise happen after the fact.
2. **A wrong build is expensive to detect.** If your failures are loud and immediate, write tests
   instead.
3. **The system outlives the conversation.** Contracts are how a decision survives into a session
   with no memory of why it was made.

For anything smaller, take the five ideas from `README.md` §6 and skip the apparatus.

## Phase 1 · Topology — read before you design

Read the existing system. Not to plan; to stop yourself proposing a rebuild of code that works.

Use an offline AST tool over the repository rather than reading files one by one — you want the
shape (what calls what, what is dead, what is load-bearing) before you have opinions.

**This phase produces no document.** It produces restraint, and restraint is the thing that stops
Phase 9 from quietly re-deriving 16,000 lines of working service code.

> **What it prevents:** the single most expensive agent failure, which is a competent rebuild of
> something that already existed and worked.

## Phase 2 · Narration — the human walks the day

Sit with the person who does the work and have them narrate it, **screen by screen, in order**.
Not a requirements interview. A walkthrough of a real day.

Ask about the exceptions as they arise — *what happens when the truck breaks down, when the crew
finds the tree dead, when it rains* — but let them drive the sequence.

**Everything in the eventual contract suite should be traceable to this phase.** If a requirement
originates from you imagining what such a system needs, mark it and check it. That is where
invented requirements enter.

> **What it prevents:** a system that is internally coherent and describes a job nobody does.

## Phase 3 · Decision trees — make the branches visible

Convert the narration into Mermaid decision trees. Every scenario, every branch, every terminal
state. Then hand them back to the human.

The whole purpose is to make it **cheap to say "no, not that branch."** Prose hides branches — a
reader nods along and the disagreement surfaces three weeks into the build. A tree cannot hide one:
every fork is drawn, and an unratified fork is visibly unratified.

This is the conversion step. Narration in, **ratified decisions** out.

> **What it prevents:** the disagreement that surfaces after the code is written, when changing it
> is a redesign rather than an edit.

## Phase 4 · Artifacts — open the real files

Get the actual spreadsheets, the actual API responses, the actual database rows. Open them. Not the
documentation of them, not a description, not a remembered shape.

**This is the phase that pays for the method.** On the reference project it produced three facts
that no amount of careful thinking would have found:

- Two files carried **different coordinate systems under identical column headers**, one with its
  axes reversed relative to its own header names.
- 594 of 4,756 asset numbers carried **leading zeros that were part of their identity** — so the
  key is a string, forever, and any integer parse silently corrupts 12% of the data.
- Seven status strings had to be reproduced **byte-exact**, including a trailing space in one of
  them, because a downstream system matched on them literally.

None of the three is inferable. All three are build-breaking. All three took one afternoon of
opening files.

**Rule:** if you cannot record a real sample of a payload the contract consumes, the contract is
not ready to be written. Go dump it first.

> **What it prevents:** a contract authored against a remembered shape, whose failure presents as a
> data-quality outlier rather than as the specification error it is.

## Phase 5 · The plan — one product authority

Write one document that holds every ratified decision from Phase 3 and every fact from Phase 4.
Call it `PLAN.md`. Number the sections so contracts can cite them.

Its job is to be **the place a decision lives**, so a contract can cite it rather than re-argue it,
and so two contracts cannot quietly ratify different versions of the same thing.

It also holds the **open items** — the decisions nobody has made yet. Those become `[ESCALATE]`
tags downstream, and they are the honest output of this phase. A plan with no open items after a
real Phase 2 is a plan that guessed.

> **What it prevents:** two contracts, both internally consistent, that disagree.

## Phase 6 · Standards — write the rules before the contracts

Copy the four documents from this repository and fill in their project slots:

| Document | Project slots to fill |
|---|---|
| `CONVENTIONS.md` | global conventions table, area codes, entity vocabulary, hard constraints |
| `DESIGN-GUIDELINE.md` | the anti-pattern table — replace the examples with defects from *your* codebase |
| `RUBRIC.md` | usually unchanged; add checks when an audit finds a gap the rubric missed |
| `ORCHESTRATION.md` | the paths the orchestrator must not write to |

`templates/PROJECT-CONVENTIONS.md` is the fill-in sheet.

**Do this before writing any contract.** Sixteen authors working against rules written afterwards
produce sixteen documents that then have to be reconciled.

> **What it prevents:** a reconciliation pass that costs more than the contracts did.

## Phase 7 · The exemplar — hand-write exactly one

Write one complete system contract yourself. By hand. All of it.

Pick the one that owns the schema, or whatever the most structurally central component is —
everything else will reference it, so getting its vocabulary right propagates.

**Sixteen authors copying one artifact produce a suite. Sixteen authors reading a style guide
produce sixteen documents.** This is the highest-leverage hour in the whole method, and there is no
way to delegate it: the exemplar *is* the specification of what a contract looks like here, and it
only works if it is genuinely good.

> **What it prevents:** a suite where every contract is individually acceptable and no two are
> shaped alike, which is unreadable in aggregate and impossible to gate consistently.

## Phase 8 · The index — built from `find`, never from memory

Build the contract index: every contract ID, its area, its owner, and its `scope_paths` — the exact
files it may write.

**Every path in that index must come from a shell command you actually ran.** `find`, `ls`, `git
ls-files`. Not from prose, not from memory, not from the plan.

This phase is numbered 8 because on the reference project it was originally done from prose, and
four claimed paths did not exist while 2,351 lines across 27 handlers ended up owned by nobody. The
rubric caught neither, because it checked that no path appeared in *two* contracts and never checked
that every required path appeared in *one*. A path in two contracts was a defect; a path in zero was
invisible. That asymmetry produced an unwinnable review before a single line of code was written.

The fix was two new rubric checks (`A1.8` path exists, `A1.9` every required path is owned) and this
phase.

> **What it prevents:** work that no contract owns, discovered when a builder needs to write a file
> and has no authority to.

## Phase 9 · Author in parallel

Dispatch one sub-agent per system contract. Each one gets, and only gets:

1. `AUTHORING-BRIEF.md` with the project slots filled;
2. the exemplar from Phase 7;
3. the four standards from Phase 6;
4. the plan from Phase 5;
5. its own row from the index in Phase 8.

Each writes **exactly one file** and no production code. Each finishes with a self-grade against
Rubric D and Rubric A, naming any defect it could not close and why.

The self-grade is not ceremony. An author that names its own unclosed defect has converted a hidden
soft spot into a tracked one, which is the entire objective.

> **What it prevents:** the serialisation that makes a large suite take weeks — and, via the shared
> brief and exemplar, the divergence that usually makes parallel authoring not worth it.

## Phase 10 · Audit — two different readers, two different blind spots

Two audits, and they are not interchangeable.

**10a · Coverage audit.** Does every requirement in the plan have a home in exactly one contract?
Run it against the plan and the index. On the reference project this found four orphaned
requirements — stories had been deleted and their requirements went with them.

**10b · The cold-read walkthrough.** This is the important one, and it is the check most people
skip.

Dispatch a fresh agent — a strong one — that reads **only the contracts**. No plan. No repository.
No access to the conversation. Ask it for one thing:

> *Write the manual. How does this finished system behave and feel, based on the contracts alone?*

Then read what comes back and look for two things: what it got **wrong**, and what it **could not
determine**. Both are specification defects, and this reader is the only one who can find them,
because it is the only reader who cannot unconsciously patch a gap from memory. You cannot do this
yourself. Neither can anyone who was in the room.

On the reference project this found **seven contradictions** that the coverage audit, the rubric
self-grades and two human passes had all missed — including two contracts declaring different
column sets for the same table, and two contracts giving coordinates for the same physical location
4 km apart.

It also found the more valuable thing, which was an **unspecified behaviour rather than a
contradiction**: nothing in the suite caused a carried-forward route to reach the crew. Every
individual contract was correct. The sequence they implied did not close. Only a reader
reconstructing the whole day end to end would notice.

> **What it prevents:** the class of defect where every contract is right and the system is wrong.

## Phase 11 · Gate — three rubrics, in order

Per contract, in this order, from `RUBRIC.md`:

1. **Rubric D — is it unambiguous?** Run first. There is no point assessing completeness on a
   document that can be read two ways.
2. **Rubric A — is it buildable?** Run before any builder is dispatched.
3. **Rubric B — did the build satisfy it?** Run on handback, by the orchestrator, never by the
   builder that produced the work.

Zero defects is the only pass. Any `[ESCALATE]` — in the contract or in anything it depends on —
blocks dispatch. **Do not resolve it by picking a default;** that is the exact failure the whole
method exists to prevent, and doing it at the gate is worse than doing it while authoring, because
by the gate everyone has stopped looking.

Then the two detectors run continuously through the build:

- **D-REPEAT** — the same contract fails twice → stop, it is a design error, not a build error. No
  third attempt on the same design, ever.
- **D-CLUSTER** — three or more defects at once across different components → do not open three
  fixes. Ask what single thing was left undesigned. It is usually an unowned lifecycle falling
  between contracts.

> **What it prevents:** grinding a fundamentally wrong design through attempt after attempt, each
> one looking like it is nearly there.

## What actually goes wrong, in order of cost

Ranked from the reference project. Every one of these happened.

| # | Failure | Phase that catches it | Cost if missed |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Contract authored against a remembered payload shape | 4 | Silent data corruption, presents as a data-quality issue |
| 2 | Work owned by no contract | 8 | Unwinnable review; builder blocked with no authority to proceed |
| 3 | Every contract correct, the implied sequence broken | 10b | A promised behaviour that never happens, discovered in production |
| 4 | Two contracts ratifying different versions of one decision | 5, 10b | Two builders build to different specs, both pass their own gate |
| 5 | A rule stated without its reason | 6 (D7) | Deleted by the next competent reader, who was not careless |
| 6 | An invariant enforced by convention rather than structure | 6 (D6) | Holds until the first agent that did not read the contract |
| 7 | Rebuild of working code | 1, 7 (disposition) | Weeks, and a worse version of what existed |
| 8 | Silently invented default for an undecided question | 6 (latitude bands) | Divergent builds, none of them wrong per the contract |

## The three habits underneath all of it

**Write for the weakest reader, not the smartest.** A senior engineer fills gaps from context you
did not write down, and the result looks like success. The gaps stay load-bearing and invisible
until a fresh session hits them. Designing for the reader who cannot infer is what makes a contract
auditable — a requirement a weak reader cannot execute is a requirement you have not specified.

**Record the mistake in the artifact.** Every phase above that carries a story got that story
because something failed. The reference project's rubric has a note explaining why `A1.9` exists.
Contracts that record their own near-misses do not get those defects re-introduced by the next
author, and they are the only durable form of institutional memory in a workflow where no
participant has a memory.

**Default to escalation.** A suite with twelve honest `[ESCALATE]` tags is fixable in an afternoon.
A suite with twelve silently-invented defaults is indistinguishable from a correct one, right up
until it is built.
